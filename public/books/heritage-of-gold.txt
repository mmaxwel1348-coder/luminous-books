n every generation, a silent crisis unfolds — the struggle of the
young to find direction, identity, and meaning. As traditional
mentorship structures weaken under the pressures of rapid
modernization, media overload, and fragmented communities,
the need to reawaken value-based guidance has never been
more urgent.

gold buried beneath the surface of confusion and fear.

faults but about unveiling greatness. It is a sacred trust — a
legacy passed from one generation to the next. The book is
a clarion call to families, churches, and communities to stop
overlooking this treasure and instead rise to protect, polish,
and present it to the world.

that mentorship is not a task reserved for the perfect, but an

within and dare to draw it out.

Introduction
I
n a world wounded by broken relationships, moral
confusion, and widening generational gaps, mentorship is
no longer a luxury—it is a lifeline. Yet, its true essence has
been forgotten. Many speak of mentorship, but few are willing
to pay the price to truly live it.
This book, a heritage of gold: Unveiling the Treasure in Our
Youth, is a bold invitation to rediscover the sacred responsibility
of walking alongside others—especially the young—with
compassion, intentionality, and spiritual conviction. It calls us
not just to bear burdens, but to recognize the treasure within
each life and commit to unveiling it with love and wisdom.
To mentor is to stand beside another person and say, “There
is treasure within you, and I will help you find it.” It is to see
potential where others see failure, to offer presence where
others withdraw. True mentorship demands selflessness, time,
and sacrifice—sometimes even pain. But more than burdenbearing, mentorship is about discovering and drawing out the
gold within each soul.
Beneath the surface of every young person lies untapped
greatness—gifts, talents, and divine purpose waiting to be
awakened. There is a heritage of gold in our youth—often
buried under fear, pain, or confusion. The mentor’s sacred role
is to help unearth it with patience, clarity, and love.
We begin this journey by defining mentorship and identifying
the attributes of a true mentor—one who is called, prepared,
and willing to walk the long road for the good of another. The
10
early chapters will help you discern that call and embrace the
qualities that make mentorship both powerful and sacred.
As we move forward, we explore mentorship across various
seasons of life: within families, in schools, in the broader
community, and from one-on-one relationships to group
influence. We examine spiritual mentorship, the power of
communication, and the sacred responsibility of shaping young
lives with insight, courage, and grace.
Then, we turn to a deeply neglected space—the plight of the
boy child. In a world rightly championing the empowerment
of girls, boys are too often forgotten, left to wrestle silently
with identity confusion, emotional suppression, and
societal neglect. This book raises a redemptive cry: that
mentorship must uplift both sons and daughters. We need
a balanced approach that nurtures the whole generation—
not just half of it—with wisdom, tenderness, and strength.
Only after this do we confront mentorship’s darker
realities—the betrayal of trust, the wolves in sheep’s
clothing, and the devastating consequences of scandal
and abuse. We unveil the subtle steps that can lead to
spiritual and emotional harm, while offering faith-based,
practical strategies to protect and heal those affected.
Whether you are a parent, teacher, church leader, youth worker,
or simply someone with a heart for the next generation, this
book is for you. It contains real stories, spiritual insight, and
a deep yearning for transformation. If you have ever felt the
nudge to walk with someone—not just to lift their load, but to
help them discover their gold—then turn the page.
There is treasure to be found.
Your mentorship journey begins here.

CHAPTER ONE
UNDERSTANDING MENTORSHIP: DEFINITION, PURPOSE
AND THE CALL
Mentorship is a relationship in which a more experienced
or knowledgeable individual guides and supports the
personal or professional growth of another.
In the context of this book, the mentor refers to an adult who
supports and nurtures younger members of society on their
journey through life. These younger individuals—referred
to interchangeably as youths, children, or mentees—are the
recipients of guidance, encouragement, and wisdom from their
mentors.
Unlike formal education, mentorship does not follow a fixed
syllabus. Each mentoring relationship is unique, shaped by the
personalities involved, the goals they set, and the context in
which the mentorship occurs.
Every person is mentored—both formally and informally—
throughout life. In turn, each of us has the potential to pass on
those insights, experiences, and skills to the next generation.
Mentorship takes place in every sphere of life: social,
professional, political, academic, and spiritual. We see
presidents grooming young leaders, seasoned professionals
guiding rising stars in their fields, and religious communities
inspiring new vocations through the example of elder members.
01
14
Many parishes, for instance, have produced numerous priests
and religious leaders simply because of the influence and
mentorship of committed parishioners.
Most of us can recall a teacher—perhaps in junior or senior
school—whose mentorship went far beyond classroom
instruction and left a lasting impact on our lives. These are just
a few of the many ways mentorship manifests in everyday life.
The attributes of a Mentor
Since each mentoring relationship is shaped by its goals, the
role of a mentor often varies. A mentor might serve different
purposes depending on the needs of the mentee and the stage of
the mentorship journey. These varying responsibilities reflect
key attributes that define effective mentors. Let’s explore some
of these attributes:
The Star / Role Model
A star shines brightly, becoming a beacon for others to follow. In
the same way, a mentor serves as a living example—a reference
point from whom the mentee can learn through observation.
Our actions, reactions, and behavior communicate volumes,
often more powerfully than words.
The Torch / Candle
“A mentor is a torch that helps kids see into their future.” — Dr.
Julie Connor
A mentor sees potential in a young person even when they
cannot see it themselves. Like a torch, a mentor illuminates
hidden talents and provides direction and hope. They inspire
dreams and help the mentee stay focused on achieving them.
“You must see in kids what they don’t see in themselves... You may be
the only hope in the lives of many of your students.” — James Vint
15
Even a small candle can light many others without losing its
flame—so too can a mentor guide many without losing their
essence.
The Bridge / Ladder / Stepping Stone
A mentor acts as a bridge, helping the mentee navigate difficult
stages of life. They offer reassurance and hope about:
- The Past: Mistakes can be corrected. One is not condemned
by past failures. There’s always room for redemption.
- The Present: They help mentees realize their worth,
despite external judgment or internal doubts.
- The Future: They instill belief in brighter days ahead,
assuring the mentee that challenges are surmountable.
Anchor / Pillar of Support / Shoulder to Lean On
Life presents emotional wounds, particularly to young people
facing family challenges or instability. A mentor fills emotional
gaps, offering identity, reassurance, and stability. They become
a safe harbor amid life’s storms.
A Friend
Beyond guidance, a mentor is a companion—someone who
walks alongside the mentee, brightening their journey.
Friendship fosters trust and allows for positive influence to
grow naturally over time.
A Teacher
A mentor educates, not just academically but about life. They
equip young people with values, courage, and perspective,
preparing them to face the world with resilience. In essence,
they give them “wings to fly.”
16
A Leader
A mentor offers direction and encourages focus. They lead by
example and provide clarity amid the confusion of life’s many
options.
An Intermediary
A mentor often acts as an intercessor—praying for, and
spiritually supporting, their mentee. They also mediate between
mentees and other key figures like parents or guardians when
needed.
A Counselor
Mentors are often the first people young individuals turn to
when facing personal issues. Their presence provides ongoing
guidance, comfort, and emotional grounding. Professional
referrals may follow, but mentorship is often the first step
toward healing.
THE CALL TO MENTORSHIP
The ‘gold in our youth”
Each young person’s heart should thus be considered holy
ground, a bearer of seeds of divine life before which we must take
off our shoes in order to draw near and enter more deeply into
mystery. — Pope Francis
This profound reflection by Pope Francis calls us to approach
the youth with reverence and humility. Their souls are sacred,
and their lives reflect the pure and unblemished image of God.
Young people are like glittering gold—shining with promise and
potential. But just as gold can lose its luster when neglected, so
too can the brilliance of youth fade if not nurtured. The prophet
Jeremiah lamented this in Lamentations 4:2:
17

they are treated like common clay pots.

their spark—just as the golden city walls grew dull in darkness.
Our youth and children are among the most valuable treasures
in families, churches, nations, and society as a whole.

Scripture repeatedly affirms the importance of youth and
children. Consider the words of Jesus:

- “ Let the children come to me... for the kingdom of heaven

Jesus’ consistent affirmation of children teaches us that they are
not only precious but also our teachers in spiritual simplicity,
openness, forgiveness, and humility.
Youthful Heroes of the Bible
The Bible is rich with stories of young people whose faith and
courage changed the course of history.
Daniel, Shadrach, Meshach, and Abednego were still

faith. In time, he rose to save nations during famine.
David, still a young shepherd boy, confronted and defeated
Goliath when seasoned warriors were paralyzed with fear. His
victory was not only physical—it was spiritual and national.
Jeremia was called by God as a prophet around the age of

say, ‘I am only a youth,’ for he would speak to nations.
Esther, taken into the palace as a young woman, risked her life
to save her people. Samuel heard God’s voice as a child and
served faithfully in the temple. Solomon became king while
still young, and asked for wisdom above all else. Mary, the
mother of Jesus, responded to the angel Gabriel’s message with
humble obedience—most likely in her early teens.
The apostles too were young when called by Jesus. Many
scholars believe they were in their late teens through to early

In more recent history, Dominic Savio, Maria Goretti, and the

At Fatima and Lourdes, the Virgin Mary chose to appear not to

Heaven’s trust.
These examples remind us that youth are not merely the

Why We Must Mentor Our Youth
To preserve the “golden” state of our young people, we must
intentionally and consistently mentor them. That is the heart
of this book.
A “golden” youth might be characterized by the following
attributes:
Spiritual (Soul): Fear of God, active participation in church,
and sincere faith.
Social (Heart): Kindness, humility, respectfulness, sociability,
and a positive reputation.
Physical (Body): Industrious, responsible, decent in speech
and dress, creative and productive.
Mental (Mind): Confidence, goal-oriented, resilient, open to
advice, adaptable to change.
Our youth are future leaders, professionals, and parents. Their
current activism against corruption and injustice reflects a
longing for involvement. Mentoring them ensures that when
our time has passed, we leave society in trustworthy hands.
Barriers to Youth Fulfillment
Many young people fail to embody these golden traits due to:

- Lack of exposure and support
- Poverty and disasters (e.g., conflict, displacement, trauma)

hidden treasure can be uncovered and restored.

CHAPTER TWO
BECOMING A MENTOR: DISCERNING THE CALL AND THE KEY
QUALITIES
Mentorship often begins with a quiet nudge—a sense
that we are meant to guide, support, or walk alongside
someone younger. But how do we recognize this call?
Who initiates it? And how do we respond when we feel drawn
to mentor someone?
The best way to understand this journey is to turn to Scripture,
where we find rich, practical examples of mentorship in action.
Mentorship in the Bible
Joseph and Jesus: God chose Joseph to raise and mentor
Jesus—not by biology, but by purpose. As Jesus’ earthly father,
Joseph nurtured Him physically, emotionally, mentally, and
spiritually. He led by quiet strength, guiding the Savior of the
world with humility and faithfulness.
Jesus and John: John, the youngest disciple, shared a deep
personal bond with Jesus. In His final moments on the cross,
Jesus entrusted John to His mother Mary—and Mary to John.
This symbolized an enduring mentorship that extended beyond
Jesus’ earthly ministry.
Moses and Joshua: God instructed Moses to pass on leadership
to Joshua, a younger man chosen to lead the Israelites into the
02
24
Promised Land. Moses mentored him patiently, teaching him
obedience, courage, and leadership. Joshua learned not just
from Moses’ words, but from walking closely beside him—
even lingering in the presence of God after Moses left (Exodus
33:11).
Paul and Timothy: Paul took young Timothy under his wing—
teaching, correcting, empowering, and encouraging him in
his faith journey and ministry. Their relationship reflects
intentional mentorship grounded in trust, accountability, and
love.
Elijah and Elisha: Elijah was instructed by God to mentor
Elisha, who followed him with steadfast loyalty. When Elijah
was taken to heaven, Elisha inherited a double portion of
his mentor’s spirit. This passing of the mantle marked the
beginning of Elisha’s powerful ministry.
Naomi and Ruth: Ruth’s deep loyalty to Naomi opened the door
to mentorship. Naomi guided her through grief, uncertainty,
and critical life decisions. Through that relationship, Ruth
found purpose—and ultimately became an ancestor of Jesus.
Who Initiates Mentorship?
The examples above show that God is always at the center of
mentorship, whether directly or indirectly;
- Sometimes God appoints the mentor or mentee directly
(e.g., Elisha to Elijah).
- Sometimes the mentor initiates (e.g., Paul choosing
Timothy).
- Other times the mentee seeks out guidance (e.g., Ruth
clinging to Naomi).
25
In our modern lives, we may not receive divine visions or
prophetic instructions—but God still works through people,
circumstances, and promptings in our hearts to bring mentormentee relationships to life.
Recognizing the Mentor’s Calling
If you are surrounded by younger people—at home, work,
church, or your neighborhood—you are already a potential
mentor. But certain situations may reveal a deeper, more
intentional call to mentor specific individuals.
Here are two ways that calling may become clear:
1. Through Personal Traits and Gifts
Talents, Values, and Profession: If your profession, personal
values, or talents align with those of a young person, chances
are you’re positioned to be a natural guide. Whether you’re a
teacher, artist, coach, counselor, or just someone deeply rooted
in life experience, mentorship often flows from grace, shared
passion and purpose.
Your talent is what you do naturally. Your grace is when God
uses it to transform someone else’s life.
Passion for the Youth: True mentorship is sustained by
passion—deep, selfless concern for the well-being and growth
of young people.
Signs of passion for the youth
You may be called to mentor if you experience a deep sense
of joy when young people succeed—even those who are
not personally connected to you. Their achievements stir
something within you, as if their victories are your own.
You also feel a sharp pain when you witness youth suffering
from injustice, neglect, or despair. Their struggles burden your
heart, and you find yourself longing to help, even in small ways.
26
There is a natural openness in you toward children and young
people from all walks of life. You do not discriminate or withhold
compassion; instead, you welcome them with genuine interest
and warmth.
You find fulfillment in guiding them without expecting anything
in return. The act of walking beside a young person and offering
direction brings a quiet satisfaction to your soul.
Your interest in youth extends even to the things that interest
them. You engage in youth-related content and conversations
not out of duty, but because you genuinely care about their
world.
There is a noticeable ease and comfort when you interact with
young people. You feel at home in their company, and they
respond positively to your presence.
Most importantly, you respect their ideas—even when those
ideas are still unformed or maturing. You recognize potential
where others see immaturity, and you treat them with dignity
and patience.
2. Through Everyday Environments
Mentorship can grow organically in your everyday settings.
Parents are naturally their children’s first mentors—guiding
them in values, behavior, and life choices from an early age.
Older siblings often take on a mentorship role, showing the
way through both words and example. Their influence, though
informal, shapes the younger ones more than they realize.
Teachers, pastors, and community leaders find themselves
mentoring through their roles. Their presence in the lives of the
youth—especially when consistent—makes a lasting impact.
27
Neighbors and family friends can also become significant
figures in a young person’s life. Sometimes, their interest and
wisdom speak into areas that even parents may not touch.
In the workplace, professionals mentor their juniors—offering
guidance, encouragement, and practical skills that shape
careers and character alike.
Sometimes, all it takes is presence and consistency. Mentorship
doesn’t always require a formal title—just a heart that sees,
listens, and shows up.
Christ didn’t wait for us to seek Him—He came to us. Let us do
the same for the youth in our lives.
Mentees can choose mentors
While mentors often feel called, mentees sometimes initiate the
relationship. Children and teens are incredibly perceptive—
they can sense which adults are genuine, safe, and wise.
They will naturally gravitate toward people who welcome
their questions, make them feel seen and valued, and listen
attentively. These qualities build the trust that mentorship
requires.
Youth seek out those who offer guidance without judgment and
who show consistent kindness. Sometimes, a smile or a gentle
word is all it takes to open the door to a meaningful connection.
Here are some examples I have personally encountered.
a) A pre-teen boy once approached me with a warm greeting
that caught both his parents and me by surprise. I responded
in kind and gave him a listening ear. He showed me a skit
he had written and asked if I could help edit it. Although I
wasn’t very skilled in English skits, I didn’t say so. Instead,
I promised to get back to him. In time, I realized he wasn’t
28
really after feedback—he simply needed a friendly mentor.
He soon became my mentee. Through mentorship, he
overcame numerous internal conflicts and today, he is a
proud graduate confidently seeking employment.
b) A teenager once opened up to me about the instability he
was experiencing at home. His story was deeply moving—
he had turned to drugs as a way of coping and was struggling
with addiction. I offered a listening ear and became a steady
source of support. I journeyed with him through those
challenging years until he outgrew his teenage struggles
and found stability. Today, he is a successful family man
working abroad.
c) Another teenager came to me, anxious about his health.
I accompanied him for a medical check-up, offering
reassurance and praying with him throughout. Thankfully,
the results were clear—he was perfectly fine. What he
truly needed was emotional support, as his parents had
separated. He became both a friend and a mentee. Through
mentorship, he regained his confidence and is now a source
of hope and encouragement to many.
d) A teenage girl who had lost her parents confided in me that
she felt rejected and struggled to find meaning in life. It was
clear that she was overwhelmed with grief, and her family
was unable to help. Through consistent encouragement
and prayer, I walked with her through those challenging
teenage years. Today, she is a cheerful and confident young
woman studying abroad.
e) A pre-teen once approached me to ask about youth activities
at church. Though he was still too young to join officially, I
shared a list of upcoming events. Later, I realized he wasn’t
particularly drawn to the activities themselves—he was
29
seeking connection. And he kept it. Through mentorship,
he was able to make informed career choices and secured
a job at a tender age.
f) During a visit to pray for a sick lady, we were informed
that her grandson was also unwell. I went to check on him,
and he later followed up with me. Over time, he became
a cherished mentee. I guided him through a season of
negative peer pressure that nearly overwhelmed him.
Today, he is a joyful university student with a part-time
job.
g) A teenage boy used to greet me faithfully after every youth
group session. Over time, our bond deepened. He eventually
became not just my mentee, but also my godson. Today, he
is a happy high school student.
The above examples demonstrate that mentees have the ability
to initiate mentorship relationships. In my experience, such
mentee-initiated relationships are often the most impactful,
and they tend to grow into long-lasting associations.
The Role of Parents in choosing mentors
Parents sometimes seek additional mentors for their children,
especially during significant transitions. This may happen
when a child is entering a new phase of life, such as after
undergoing initiation rites or graduating from school. These
moments often come with new challenges and uncertainties
that require guidance beyond what parents alone can offer.
In single-parent households or families facing emotional strain,
children may also need extra emotional support from a trusted
adult. A mentor can provide a safe space to process their
feelings and gain perspective. Similarly, when a child begins to
explore a specific career path or embark on a spiritual journey,
30
having a knowledgeable and supportive mentor can make all
the difference.
Sometimes, parents recognize that their child may benefit
from additional guidance or even counseling—someone to
offer objective wisdom and direction. In today’s world, where
trust is more fragile than ever, it is essential for parents to
choose mentors carefully, prioritizing integrity and character.
If multiple families approach you to guide their children, take it
as a strong confirmation that you possess the gift of mentorship.
When Others Facilitate Mentorship
Teachers, pastors, and youth leaders often recognize
mentorship needs. They may step in themselves or connect
youth with adults who are a better fit.
If you are approached and feel unqualified, remember this: God
doesn’t call the qualified—He qualifies the called.
You did not choose me, but I chose you and appointed you to
go and bear fruit—fruit that will last. — John 15:16
Mentorship is not about having all the answers. It’s about
showing up, walking with someone, and letting God work
through you.
QUALITIES OF A SUCCESSFUL MENTOR
Each mentor is unique, shaped by their personality, experiences,
and values. However, there are fundamental qualities that
every effective mentor should cultivate. These are virtues we
can pray for and practice in order to support young people
meaningfully.
1. Kindness
Kindness is more than being nice—it is a lifestyle of generosity,
compassion, and warmth. A single act of kindness can spark a
chain reaction, lifting spirits and transforming communities. In
31
Scripture, kindness is expressed through merciful and selfless
acts. The Good Samaritan is a timeless example—offering care

THE SACRIFICE OF MENTORSHIP
To sacrifice means giving up something valuable for the

Sacrifice does not come naturally. As outlined in Value Investing and

Human beings are inherently self-focused—thinking, feeling,

quote puts it:
“Everyone thinks about themselves; I’m the only one who thinks
about me.”- Anonymous
But this instinct becomes a barrier to mentorship when we
only help those who benefit us in return—our own children,
relatives, or friends. Effective mentorship means choosing to
help those who may never repay us. It’s stepping outside of
our comfort zones and entering someone else’s world with
empathy and love.

Ambition drives us to strive, succeed, and stay busy. But

mentorship becomes a neglected calling.
Many parents are too “busy” to spend time with their children.
Many adults fail to mentor simply because their schedules are

“What we do for ourselves dies with us. What we do for others

Mentorship demands intentional time and presence. The

Greed whispers: “Only share what you don’t need.”

FAMILY MENTORSHIP
In this chapter, we focus on family mentorship, which includes
parents, siblings, and extended family—each playing a
distinct yet interconnected role in shaping character and life
perspective.
The parents
Parents are a child’s first and most influential mentors—
beginning from the moment of conception. A child inherits not
only physical traits but also values, beliefs, and behaviors from
their parents. They provide the earliest point of identity, and
their influence forms the foundation for all future mentorship.
Children are most comfortable with their parents, and as
such, parents must strive to make the home a safe, stable,
and nurturing environment. Good parenting lays a firm
groundwork that enables easier mentoring later in life, while
poor parenting often creates gaps that require complex and
sometimes professional intervention, such as counseling,
therapy, or rehabilitation.
A Swahili proverb aptly states: “Usipojenga ufa utajenga
ukuta”—if you do not fix a small crack, you will eventually
be forced to rebuild the wall. This emphasizes the need for
consistent, intentional parenting.
Your children are your most valuable assets—your living
legacy. While every parent is unique and none are perfect,
there are universal principles that guide effective parenting as
mentorship. Below are some roles parents play as mentors:
i) The parent as a role model
Children learn by observing more than by listening. Parents are
watched closely and imitated—whether they realize it or not.
47
“Don’t worry that children never listen to you; worry that they
are always watching you.” — Robert Fulghum.
For example, a father who drops his family at church but
waits in the car sends a subtle message. His sons may grow
up believing that church is not a man’s responsibility. These
unspoken lessons shape convictions that are hard to reverse.
ii) The Parent as a Torch
Parents are uniquely positioned to recognize their children’s
talents and weaknesses early in life. As mentors, they help
nurture strengths and address areas of growth, lighting the
path ahead for the child.
iii) The Parent as a Stepping Stone
Parents provide for their children’s physical, emotional, and
educational needs at every stage of life. They serve as the
ladder through which a child climbs toward independence.
iv) The Parent as a Pillar of Support
Parents are a child’s emotional anchor. They offer reassurance,
comfort, and stability, especially during emotionally volatile
periods. Understanding the emotional shifts children
experience at different ages is vital.
v) The Parent as a Friend
Though friendship and parenting serve different roles, warmth,
empathy, and connection are essential in the parenting
relationship. In her article ‘Parent Vs. friend: Navigating the
delicate balance”, Anna Kaminsky states the dangers of being
your child’s friend. Not only does it impair your ability to
guide and set rules, but most importantly, it creates anxiety in
the child who feels pressured to act like an adult before they
are ready. However, in her conclusion titled “drawing the line
between friendship and parenting’, Anna observes as follows:
48
‘Not being your child’s friend doesn’t mean you have to be a
cold or distant parent. On the contrary, by not placing adult
expectations on your child, you’ll free up more time to relax, have
fun together, and be attentive to your child’s needs and feelings.
You and your child will both benefit from this clear division in
roles, without sacrificing the bond you share.’
This healthy bonding can still be termed ‘friendly parenting’.
Friendly parenting means accepting each child’s uniqueness
and embracing them as they are. It involves correcting with
love rather than punishment, nurturing the child’s talents, and
offering protection that is neither smothering nor neglectful.
A friendly parent is present—available to listen, guide, and
simply share life. And just as importantly, they find joy in
spending time together, creating memories through laughter,
play, and shared experiences.
(vi) The Parent as a Teacher
Parents are a child’s first and most enduring teachers, shaping
their development through patience, presence, and example.
From basic life skills to complex life lessons, children learn best
by observing and participating. When a mother consistently
engages in household chores, children naturally begin to
understand that they, too, have a role to play in maintaining the
home. Without this modeling, they may grow up thinking such
tasks are solely the responsibility of a house help. Similarly,
when a father actively takes part in domestic duties, sons learn
that helping at home is not only acceptable for men—it is
expected. These experiences build character and prepare them
for independent living in the future. It’s also valuable to have
a variety of tools available at home and to teach children how
to use them. For instance, involving them in changing a car tire
or fixing simple items can foster confidence, responsibility, and
49
practical life skills. Such shared tasks leave a lasting positive
impact.
(vii) The Parent as a Leader
Parental leadership involves resilience, sacrifice, and direction.
The authoritative parenting style—firm yet compassionate—
promotes discipline and emotional security.
viii) The Parent as an Intermediary
Parents are spiritual mentors and intercessors. Involving
children in prayer, worship, charity, and speaking blessings
over them instills lasting moral values.
Sibling Mentorship
First-borns typically learn directly from parents, while younger
siblings often learn from their older brothers and sisters as
well. Siblings mentor one another naturally, just by spending
time together.
Though first-borns are influential, learning also occurs
from younger siblings who have unique experiences and
talents. Parents must ensure all children are seen and guided
impartially.
Extended Family Mentorship
Traditionally, raising a child was seen as a shared responsibility.
The extended family—grandparents, aunts, uncles, and
cousins—played vital roles in shaping a young person’s
identity, values, and behavior. These family bonds offered a
sense of belonging and accountability that extended beyond
the immediate household.
Today, however, the involvement of extended relatives has
become less common due to changing societal structures and
lifestyles. Yet their wisdom, presence, and influence remain
deeply valuable. To restore trust and revive the power of
50
extended family mentorship, we must be intentional. This
means being willing to mentor young relatives, opening our
homes to those in need, and upholding strong moral values
in all our relationships. The extended family still holds the

Neighborhood Mentorship

Your mentorship begins right where you are. Don’t wait to

The Special Nature of Neighborhood Mentorship
Children are keen observers of how adults treat them. They
gravitate toward the warm-hearted and avoid the cold-hearted.
A simple wave or kind greeting means a lot to a child and helps
build trust.
Never splash mud at the kids you bypass in your neighborhood

As children enter pre-teen years, they begin to withdraw

mistaken for rebellion or aloofness, and unfortunately, many

need us most—not as hovering figures, but as steady, patient
supporters who remain nearby.
By post-teenage years, youth often emerge more focused and
purpose-driven. They begin to recognize and appreciate the
adults who stood by them during teen age. These mentors
become lifelong friends. Those who walk away during a teen’s
difficult years miss out on this incredible transformation.

1. Teenage years are like a narrow bridge across a turbulent

instead of the chaos beneath.

2. Teens are energetic and curious. Without guidance, they

3. With increased freedom from parents, teens may

4. They seek external validation. If they find caring adults, they
gain confidence. If not, they may become lost or misled.
Teenagers long for adults who believe in them—who
listen, validate, encourage, and guide. A caring adult in the
neighborhood can be a powerful source of strength and
affirmation. Such a mentor instills confidence in the youth and

Teenage is a passing cloud. Let’s be part of the story of as many

Institutional Mentorship
As children enter school, they gain access to mentorship from
teachers, peers, and classmates. Education shapes their minds,
and institutions shape their character.
Children well-mentored at home usually transition more easily
into school life. Teachers must support all students equally, and
be especially mindful of those facing emotional or economic
hardship.
I recall two bright high school classmates whose potential was
lost to drugs. One faced pressure from home; the other was
grieving. Sadly, their emotional needs went unnoticed by the
teachers, and both now struggle with addiction.
53
Churches also play a mentorship role. Through spiritual

Institutions can offer individual or group mentorship.
Professionals use these platforms—like school clubs or church

careers.
Transitional Mentorship
Traditionally, the transition from childhood to adulthood
involved community mentorship during cultural initiations.
Today, that role is mostly filled by churches and professional
mentorship groups. While helpful, these one-time efforts often
lack follow-up.
A more sustainable approach is appointing a transitional
mentor—an adult the youth trusts and the parents approve of.
This mentor becomes a confidant, guide, and sounding board
during key life stages.
These relationships don’t have to be emotionally intense. A few
thoughtful gestures—remembering birthdays or celebrating
achievements—can go a long way in maintaining a strong,
supportive bond.
Profession-Based Mentorship
We all belong to different professions and interact with people
daily. Treat everyone—including young people—with respect,
regardless of their status.
Mentorship isn’t just for clients. The most critical responsibility
is guiding younger members in your own profession. Success is
greater when shared.

through school in exchange for their help during breaks.
It also means mentoring junior staff in the workplace,
recognizing and rewarding their growth, and participating in
career talks or youth empowerment programs. These acts not
only uplift individuals—they strengthen entire communities.
Let us walk with the youth—not ahead of them, not behind

SPECIAL MENTORSHIP: ADDRESSING SPECIAL NEEDS
Emotional Support Mentorship
This form of mentorship is among the most demanding,
requiring deep empathy and emotional closeness. It is vital for
children and youth with emotional gaps—gaps that often stem
from their family backgrounds.
As the number of emotionally needy youth continues to rise,
so does the importance of compassionate mentors. Below, we
explore various family setups and the emotional challenges
they present.
1. Stable and Complete Families
A family is considered complete when both parents are
physically present, and stable when they live in harmony.
Children in such homes tend to be emotionally balanced and
easier to mentor.
2. Stable but Incomplete Families
This family type involves one parent raising children in a
peaceful setting—often due to the loss of a spouse, separation,
pregnancy out of wedlock or choice.
Many children are born outside marriage especially in some
countries and many grow up with one parent or in a large or
reconstituted family context- Pope Francis.
06
56
A 2012 Pan-African study by two Canadian Sociologists
revealed that Kenya had one of the highest numbers of children
born out of wedlock in Africa. A Kenyan woman by then had a
59.5% chance of being a single mother by the age of 45 either
through pre-marital birth or dissolution of a union.
While many single parents do their best, children still yearn for
the love and guidance of the absent parent.
Research has shown the emotional risks of missing father
figures, especially among boys. In fact, many young men in
juvenile systems lacked a stable paternal presence. Responsible
adults must work alongside single parents to provide balance
and support.
Even Jesus, in His divine plan, was raised with the presence
of a father figure—Joseph—demonstrating the need for both
parental roles.
3. Stable but Partially Incomplete Families
In these families, both parents are present in name but may
not live together or be fully available. Examples include longdistance relationships, parental illness, or unengaged stepparents. Such children often lack sufficient parental bonding
and emotional presence, leading to mentorship needs from
nearby adults.
4. Complete but Unstable Families
This is one of the most harmful environments for children.
Marital conflict, addiction, abuse, and neglect create trauma that
may require both mentorship and professional intervention.
Mentors should be trained in child protection and know how
to connect with support networks.
57
5. Orphans
Children who have lost either parents—or their only parent—
require strong emotional anchors. These mentors must be
trustworthy, empathetic, and serve as both supporters and
protectors.
Unfortunately, some predators exploit orphaned children. It’s
crucial for extended families and communities to ensure these
vulnerable youth are in safe, nurturing environments.
Identifying Emotionally Needy Youth
To mentor effectively, one must be alert and compassionate.
Here are three approaches to recognize and support emotionally
needy youth:
1. Open Your Heart
Be receptive. With kindness and spiritual awareness, mentors
can sense when a young person is emotionally distressed. In
fact, the heart of an emotionally needy youth and that of the
responsive care giver will converge easily without much effort.
This is part of the wonders of the grace of God. Such a caring
adult becomes a turning point in that youth’s life.
Grieving children also need consistent outreach to process
loss. Without this, they may fall into depression, substance use,
or worse.
2. Open Your Ears
When a youth confides in you, it signals their awareness of a
problem, desire for help, and trust in you as a mentor. Never
turn them away.
Listen attentively and patiently. Often, this simple act alone
brings immense healing. If referred by a parent, approach the
child independently to gain honest insight.
58
3. Open Your Eyes
Some signs of emotional distress include withdrawal, mood
swings, neglect of hygiene, and pessimism. Left unchecked, this
can lead to substance use or suicidal thoughts.
These youth rarely seek help on their own—we must seek
them out, just as God seeks us in our brokenness.
Finance-Based Mentorship
Many youth also face material needs—orphans, children from
poor households, or those in underserved communities. In
mentoring them, we may occasionally offer material support.
Scripture encourages us not to turn away from the needy
(Sirach 4:5) and to be like a father to the fatherless (Sirach
4:10).
However, financial support must be structured carefully. As
Paul warned in 1Timothy 6:10, money can lead to harmful
outcomes if not handled wisely. ‘The love of Money is the root
cause of all evils’.
Examples of appropriate support include: paying school fees
directly, helping with uniforms or stationery, fostering a child,
or contributing through orphanages. All assistance should be
transparent and accountable.
Risks of Cash Handouts
Risks to the Mentee
When mentorship becomes entangled with unstructured
financial support, the mentee may face several risks. Some
youth may become manipulative or overly dependent, expecting
handouts rather than growth. Sudden or unplanned financial
gifts can even lead to harmful behaviors such as addiction or
irresponsible spending.
59
In some cases, mentees may feel obligated to return favors,
leaving them vulnerable to manipulation or abuse by predators.
They may also begin to trust unworthy adults who lure them
with cash; after all their mentors give them cash too.
Risks to the Mentor
Mentors, too, are not immune to risks. You may find yourself
used or manipulated by those who are more interested in
money than growth. Some parents may begin to view you as a
financial provider rather than a guide, exploiting your goodwill.
Worse still, secretive or misunderstood interactions—
especially around money—can damage your reputation. If
expectations aren’t clearly managed, even your personal safety
or peace of mind could be at stake.
Risks to Parents
Parents may also suffer unintended consequences. A child who
receives financial support elsewhere may begin to look down
on their own ‘poor’ parents, leading to disrespect or rebellion.
Secrecy around mentorship interactions can create mistrust
and sow conflict within the family unit.
Conclusion
In essence, mentorship must be clearly distinguished from
financial dependence. Generosity is noble, but when giving
is unstructured or excessive, it can undermine the purpose
of mentorship and damage the very relationships it aims to
nurture.

CHAPTER SEVEN
DEPTH AND DURATION OF MENTORSHIP
Depth of mentorship
Mentorship depth refers to the level of influence a mentor
has in a mentee’s life. It ranges from minimal to deeply
transformative. Here are the four primary levels:
a) Role Modeling
At the most foundational level, mentorship can occur simply
through observation. Young people often admire individuals
based on their behavior, discipline, and values—without direct
interaction. Many clergy members, for instance, have traced
their vocations back to role models they quietly observed in
their communities.
b) Day-to-Day Activities and Conversations
Everyday interactions with adults—through family, neighborhoods, schools, or church—often carry moments of informal guidance. Encouraging words or shared wisdom in these moments can
have a lasting impact on youth.
c) Professional Engagement
Adults serving as teachers, lecturers, pastors, or supervisors
regularly interact with youth in their professions. These
platforms provide strong mentorship opportunities, especially
when the adult intentionally nurtures and guides the younger
person’s growth.
07
62
d) Specific Mentorship Areas
This level involves a deeper, more intentional mentorship
based on specific needs. Examples include:
- Supporting youth during developmental transitions (e.g.,
adolescence to adulthood)
 - Mentoring youth in our profession
- Mentoring youth from incomplete or unstable families
- Providing psychological support where emotional or
behavioral concerns arise
In some cases, the mentor must engage or refer the mentee to a
professional counselor. Post-rehabilitation or post-counseling
mentorship plays a critical role in long-term healing and
development.
Duration of mentorship
The length of a mentorship relationship depends on its purpose.
It can be categorized as follows:
One-Off Mentorship
This involves a single interaction or a few sessions aimed at
addressing a specific concern. Group mentorship may take the
form of a one-day seminar, while individual mentorship might
consist of offering guidance or support in a moment of need.
Short-Term Mentorship
Short-term mentorship extends over weeks or months. It may
be used to help an individual adjust to a new phase of life,
navigate a short-term problem, or train for a specific task. The
relationship ends once the need is met.
63
Long-Term Mentorship
Long-term mentorship is an ongoing relationship that can span
several years—or even a lifetime. It goes beyond short-term
goals and becomes a steady presence through various seasons
of life.
This kind of mentorship may involve walking with a young
person through their developmental stages, offering consistent
guidance and support as they mature. It could look like being
a dependable father figure to a boy raised by a single mother,
or journeying alongside someone battling addiction, offering
encouragement and accountability in tandem with professional
treatment.
Long-term mentors don’t just offer advice—they offer stability,
presence, and the power of enduring commitment.

CHAPTER EIGHT
COMMUNICATION AND COUNSELING IN MENTORSHIP
The chapters we’ve explored so far have helped us understand
what mentorship entails and appreciate that everyone is
called to participate in it. Through various categorizations,
each reader can find themselves involved in one or more
forms of mentorship. Although the journey demands sacrifice,
the benefits are truly rewarding—making the cultivation of
mentorship qualities a worthy pursuit.
In the following chapters, we delve into the mentorship process
itself. While mentorship journeys vary across individuals,
certain core elements remain consistent.
Stages of the mentorship process of an individual
The journey of mentoring an individual has been described in
different ways by various writers, mentors, and psychologists.
For clarity, I will adopt the model by Sam Cook, who outlines
five key stages:
1. Rapport Building
2. Setting Direction
3. Progression
4. Winding Down
5. Moving On
07
66
While these steps may also apply in group mentorship, our
focus here is on individual mentorship. Let us explore each
stage:
1. Rapport building
Rapport is the mutual understanding and empathy that allows
for open and effective communication. It’s a bond that fosters
trust, harmony, and ease between mentor and mentee.
Establishing rapport helps both parties to appreciate each
other’s identity, values, views, talents, hobbies, and background.
This understanding enables the mentor to navigate the mentee’s
world and vice versa. Strong rapport encourages the mentee to
open up, setting the stage for a meaningful mentorship journey.
Whether initiated by the mentor, mentee, or a third party, it’s
ultimately the mentor’s responsibility to guide the relationship
toward success. Rapport building isn’t a one-time event—
it’s a gradual process that requires time, conversations, and
shared experiences. As rapport strengthens, the involvement
of parents or caretakers may also evolve.
Interestingly, some emotional needs of the mentee may be
resolved simply through the trust and reassurance built in this
phase.
Techniques for Building and Maintaining Rapport:
Building strong rapport is essential to effective mentorship.
It creates a foundation of trust, safety, and mutual respect
that allows growth to flourish. This begins with effective
communication—both speaking clearly and listening
attentively. Active listening is especially powerful when it’s
marked by empathy, undivided attention, calmness, and
positive body language.
67
Mentors build connection by sharing relevant personal
experiences, always with wisdom and discretion. Praying
for and with the mentee can deepen emotional and spiritual
bonds. Authenticity and approachability help the mentee feel
safe, while mutual respect fosters a balanced and honorable
relationship.
Trust is further reinforced by maintaining confidentiality
and consistently offering affirmation through praise and
encouragement. Setting realistic expectations, being flexible,
and avoiding judgment also help create a supportive
environment.
Finally, a mentor must stay equipped—spiritually, emotionally,
and intellectually. Core traits like kindness, humility, patience,
and commitment are not just desirable—they are essential for
meaningful, lasting rapport.
A helpful list of do’s and don’ts:
- Guide, but don’t control.
- Praise, but don’t pamper.
- Correct, but don’t condemn.
- Be flexible, but don’t aim to please.
- Affirm, but don’t tease.
- Admonish, but don’t shame.
- Be firm, but not rigid.
- Challenge, but don’t criticize.
- Be authoritative, but not authoritarian.
- Be childlike, but not childish.
68
2. Setting the direction
Once rapport is established, the mentorship officially begins.
This step involves answering the “why,” “what,” “how,” “where,”
and “when” of the process:
- Why/What: Understand and agree on the mentorship goals.
- How: Determine the method and depth of mentorship.
- Where: Choose appropriate meeting venues.
- When: Establish a flexible but clear timeline.
This is also the stage where rules are outlined and roles are
clarified.
3. Progression
The mentorship process is now underway. The mentor offers
guidance and emotional support, while the mentee actively
engages with the journey. Solutions gradually emerge—
sometimes from the mentor, sometimes from the mentee, and
often collaboratively.
4. Winding down
When the mentorship goals are achieved, the relationship
begins to wind down. Both parties reflect on the journey and
assess how the relationship may evolve in the future.
5. Moving on
This stage marks a transition from structured mentorship to an
informal relationship. The mentor may assume a more passive
role, stepping in occasionally when needed. Many of these
relationships blossom into lasting friendships or professional
connections.
69
COUNSELLING AND MENTORSHIP
As we’ve seen, mentors often take on a counseling role when
needed. It’s helpful for mentors to acquire basic counseling
skills—not to become professionals, but to know when to
assist and when to refer.
Counseling is a personal helping process where the counselor
supports the counselee through change or emotional healing.
Comparing Mentorship and Counseling (Brad Hambrick’s
distinctions):
- Mentorship is informal and long-term; counseling is formal
and short-term.
- Mentorship is holistic; counseling is problem-focused.
- Mentorship is guided by character; counseling is guided by
expertise.
- Mentorship is free; counseling is usually paid.
- Mentors are harder to find; counselors are easier to access
through referrals.
- Mentorship occurs even in normal life; counseling focuses
on resolving problems.
Stages of a counseling process (Egan’s Model):
1. Relationship Building
2. Problem Assessment
3. Goal Setting
4. Intervention
5. Evaluation and Termination
These stages mirror Sam Cook’s mentorship model, further
demonstrating the synergy between mentorship and
counseling.
70
Basic counseling skills
Effective mentorship often requires the use of basic counseling
skills to foster trust, safety, and emotional connection. One
foundational area is attending skills, often summarized by the
acronym S.O.L.E.R.: Sit upright to show attentiveness, adopt an
Open posture to appear welcoming, Lean slightly forward to
express interest, maintain appropriate Eye contact, and remain
Relaxed to help the mentee feel at ease.
Questioning skills are also essential. Open-ended questions—
such as those beginning with what, how, or why—encourage
deeper reflection and conversation. Mentors should also probe
gently with follow-up questions and use lead-ins like “You
said…” to guide mentees into greater self-awareness and clarity.
Active listening involves eliminating distractions, being fully
present, and tuning in to both verbal and nonverbal cues,
including tone of voice and body language. These clues often
reveal more than words alone.
Empathy allows the mentor to enter into the emotional world
of the mentee—to understand and share in what they are
feeling. It’s not about fixing problems immediately but being
emotionally available and compassionate.
Confidentiality is another cornerstone of trust. Mentors must
maintain strict privacy about what is shared, except in cases
where there’s a threat to life or safety. Clear boundaries around
confidentiality reassure mentees that they are safe to open up.
Finally, play therapy can be a powerful tool, particularly when
mentoring children or individuals with psychiatric concerns.
Using creative resources such as art, drawings, or toys allows
mentees to express themselves when words may fall short.
71
With these tools, a mentor can respond effectively when the
need for counseling arises—knowing when to assist and when
to involve a professional.
COMMUNICATION IN MENTORSHIP
Communication is the act of imparting or exchanging
information through speaking, writing, or other methods. When
done effectively, it enables the clear sharing of ideas, emotions,
and intentions. In mentorship, communication plays a central
role in building trust and creating meaningful relationships.
This discussion focuses primarily on verbal communication.
Speaking goes hand in hand with listening. In fact, a well-timed,
genuine conversation can be deeply healing and encouraging
for both mentor and mentee. We have already explored the
importance of listening and speaking skills within a mentorship
or counseling context. While speaking, mentors are encouraged
to use practical, relatable examples from their own lives—but
without excessive self-disclosure. It’s important for mentors
to remember that their lives are not perfect blueprints.
Sharing personal failures, in fact, can be more powerful than
successes, as it allows mentees to learn from mistakes. A bit
of vulnerability demonstrates authenticity and fosters a safe
space for openness. Often, the most impactful mentorship
occurs when both triumphs and struggles are shared.
Models of communication
Various scholars and practitioners have proposed models for
communication among mentors, mentees, and caregivers.
These models vary depending on the subject matter, process,
and dynamics involved. Here, I share three practical models
based on my personal experience and observation.
72

In this model, information flows freely among the mentor, the
mentee, and the parent or guardian.
This model applies to routine life matters where confidentiality
and emotional sensitivity are minimal. The subject is open to
discussion by all parties.
For instance, a student preparing to join college or organize
a graduation ceremony may involve both their parents and
mentor openly. All three parties can even meet together. Any
of them can initiate or drive the conversation. The mentor
and parent may consult each other to support the mentee’s
preparations.

mentorship often stalls.
4. Fear of self-expression: Without strong rapport, the
mentee may shut down emotionally.
5. Over-disclosure: While vulnerability builds trust,

6. Misleading language: Mentors should be clear and firm.
For instance, don’t sugarcoat the dangers of alcohol—speak

8. Lack of time: Inconsistent engagement weakens bonds.

9. Instability in the mentor: Emotional or mental instability

10.Lack of communication skills: Poor delivery can stall

11.Over-dominance: A mentor who monopolizes the

The mentee’s interests must take priority.
CONVERSATION IN MENTORSHIP
A conversation is an informal exchange of thoughts or
news. Mentor-mentee relationships involve numerous
conversations—some casual, others deep. While counseling
usually centers around a specific problem, a conversation can
be broader and more spontaneous. Additionally:
1. Counseling is more structured, while conversations may
arise naturally or be initiated by one party.
2. Counseling demands professionalism, while conversations
are generally relaxed and informal.
3. Conversations can address general life issues, not just
problems as in the case of counseling.
Stages of a mentorship conversation
While everyday conversations among friends may not follow
a structure, those within mentorship benefit from thoughtful
organization. Without it, conversations may become idle or
even harmful. A loosely guided structure ensures focus and
productivity.
77
According to Natalie Boyd, a productive conversation has five
stages:
1. Opening – Greetings and introductions.
2. Feed forward – A brief preview of the topic.
3. Business – The main discussion.
4. Feedback – Summary and reflection.
5. Closing – Ending the session.
A typical mentorship conversation begins with greetings
and a check-in. In cases where the mentor and mentee are
unfamiliar, introductions and small talk where each party talks
briefly about themselves help build rapport. Even in familiar
relationships, a period of separation can create distance that
must be bridged again. Genuine interest and attentiveness
break the ice and reestablish connection.
Once comfort is achieved—evidenced by natural expressions
like smiles and relaxed body language—the conversation flows
freely. Either party may bring up a topic, whether pre-planned
or spontaneous. The mentor’s role is to guide, clarify, and
provide insight. After thoughtful discussion, both should reflect
on what they’ve learned before bringing the conversation to a
close.
“Peace begins with a smile.” – St. Teresa of Kolkata
Signs of a successful conversation
A successful mentorship conversation should end on a
high note—leaving both the mentor and the mentee feeling
enriched, understood, and eager for their next encounter. It is
in these closing moments that the impact of the conversation
becomes evident.
78
The mentee walks away feeling seen and valued. They know
they were understood, appreciated, and reassured that
someone genuinely cares about their journey. There’s a sense
of emotional safety—they don’t feel alone or abandoned, but
supported and grounded.
For the mentor, there’s a deep sense of connection. They feel as
though they’ve unlocked the mentee’s heart and gained a better
understanding of their thoughts and emotions. Assumptions
are either confirmed or gently corrected. Even more rewarding
is the knowledge that they’ve helped the mentee gain a clearer
or healthier perspective.
The greatest fulfillment often comes later—when the mentee
returns, not just with progress updates, but with a heart full of
gratitude for the consistent presence of someone who believed
in them.

GROUP MENTORSHIP AND DYNAMICS
Group mentorship involves guiding young people collectively
rather than individually. It is commonly practiced in institutions
like schools and churches, as well as in community settings
such as neighborhoods and workplaces.
Even though mentorship is carried out in a group setting,
mentors should strive to understand each mentee on a personal
level. Learning their names, backgrounds, personalities,
and individual needs is crucial. When multiple mentors are
available, they can divide the group and mentor a few youths
more closely. This approach shares responsibilities while
ensuring each mentee receives meaningful attention.
Categories of characters
Understanding group dynamics is essential when working with
youth. One important aspect is recognizing different personality
types, often shaped by upbringing and temperament. Each
type has its strengths and challenges, and mentors should
be equipped to draw out their potential while helping them
overcome obstacles. Here are four common character types:
1) The Vibrant
Vibrant youth are energetic, outgoing, and confident. They
often become the stars of the group, bringing enthusiasm and
life to every gathering. They boldly volunteer for challenging
09
80
tasks and are usually quick to represent the group. Mentors
often feel reassured by their presence, especially during publicspeaking moments.
However, these youths tend to be involved in multiple activities
across different groups, which make them less consistent. Their
busy schedules mean they may not always be present when
needed. While their talents should be appreciated and utilized
when available, the group must be willing to let them go when
they have other commitments. Their lack of consistency may
also limit deep friendships within the group. It also limits their
ability to serve as long term group leaders.
2) The Moderate
These individuals possess a balanced energy. They are calm,
composed, and avoid the spotlight. Despite their quiet nature,
they are deeply committed to the group and often become its
steady foundation.
Once given a task, they take full ownership and deliver
consistently. Because of their reliability and sense of belonging,
they make excellent long-term leaders and are usually wellliked within the group.
3) The Reserved
Reserved youth prefer to stay in the background and often avoid
active participation. They may have few or no close friends. Yet,
they still crave recognition and acceptance.
Mentors must handle them with wisdom and sensitivity.
It’s a delicate balance between respecting their nature and
gently encouraging involvement so they don’t feel isolated. If
mishandled, they may quietly withdraw—and winning them
back can be extremely difficult.
81
In some cases, extreme withdrawal may signal emotional
needs. A caring mentor who earns their trust may discover
a lifelong friendship. If such youth eventually opt to leave,
mentors should continue to follow up, respecting their space
while looking for opportunities to mentor them one-on-one.
4) The Rebellious / Problematic / Deviant
These youths resist authority and break norms. While they
may seem disruptive, many are influential and can sway the
group toward mischief—a phenomenon often driven by mob
psychology and peer pressure.
In psychology, rebellion is often a form of communication.
These youth may be acting out because of unresolved pain,
lack of love, or a desire for attention. Deep down, they may feel
unloved or misunderstood.
When such youth encounter a mentor who shows genuine care,
they are often surprised—and sometimes transformed. They
can evolve into positive influencers. To help them, mentors
should:
- Seek to understand their background and the root cause
of their behavior.
- Address them individually, never reprimanding them
publicly. This personal respect earns their trust.
- Give them attention and truly listen. Parental involvement
at home also reduces rebellion.
- Publicly affirm their small victories but offer corrective
feedback privately.
- Entrust them with responsibilities—they will rise to the
occasion when they feel believed in.
82
- Treat them as normal youth, never as outcasts.
- Pray for them regularly. Divine intervention can reach
where human effort cannot.

THE BOY CHILD DEBATE: EMERGING VOICES IN
MENTORSHIP
In recent years, a growing debate has emerged around the
plight of the boy child, sparked by grave concern over the
state of modern boys. Several worrying trends have fueled
this concern, highlighting a silent crisis that has long gone
unnoticed. These include:
1. A higher number of boys ending up in juvenile prisons and
mental health institutions compared to girls.
2. Increasing indecisiveness among boys, who struggle to
initiate or complete tasks.
3. A disturbing rise in teenage male suicide cases.
4. The vulnerability of boys to recruitment into drug use,
trafficking, same-sex relationships, and other risky
behaviors.
5. A growing number of boys being lured into relationships
with older women (“sugar mummies”).
6. Young men who are unable or unwilling to manage family
businesses or protect family property.
7. Infertility in young men due to chronic alcoholism.
8. The rise of “baby daddies”—young men who start families
but have no idea how to support or lead them, often leaving
the burden to their parents.

88
9. Adult sons who are unable or unwilling to leave their
mothers’ homes and live independently.
The evolution of the girl child
I grew up in a time when girls were expected to be quiet and
submissive. Culture taught them that silence was a sign of
virtue — a hallmark of a “good girl” and a “good wife.” Male
chauvinism was the norm, and few dared to encourage girls
to dream beyond early marriage. Many girls were married off
soon after completing their primary school education, their
potential cut short.
The story of Chebet
When I was in Class Seven, our school drama team received
an exciting opportunity to perform a play at the Kenya
Broadcasting Corporation (KBC), then the national broadcaster.
The trip to Nairobi was scheduled for the December holidays,
and we eagerly looked forward to it, practicing tirelessly. As
the school term ended, drama club members were asked to
remain behind and continue rehearsals instead of traveling to
their rural homes.
We all admired Chebet, the school’s best actor and the lead in
our play, Nyamgondho Wuod Ombare. The first two practice
sessions went smoothly. Then came the third — anticipation
was high, but something felt off. Our playwright, Mr. Oduor,
was unusually late, and Chebet was nowhere to be seen. Our
excitement began to wane.
Eventually, Mr. Oduor appeared at the school gate, walking
slowly, his face clouded with sorrow. We returned to our seats
in silence, sensing that something was terribly wrong. He
entered the room quietly and greeted us with a heavy “Good
evening,” before delivering the blow: Chebet would not be
89
joining us anymore. We had to find a replacement or cancel the
play altogether.
It was a devastating announcement — we were only days away
from our trip. Thankfully, Mr. Oduor found a replacement, and
the trip went on as planned. But the tragedy lingered: Chebet’s
education and promising talent in drama had come to an abrupt
end. Her father had insisted that she return to their rural home
for initiation and marriage alongside her peers. Mr. Oduor
tried everything to change his mind, but the pleas fell on deaf
ears. I remember his anger and frustration as he condemned
the retrogressive culture that stole Chebet’s future.
Chebet’s story is just one among many. Every reader likely
knows a similar — or even worse — example. These stories
still make headlines, particularly in certain communities.
We owe much to the government and women’s rights activists
who championed the cause of the girl child. A past president
established girls’ high schools across the country and strongly
advocated for gender equity. Women’s organizations emerged
to promote the social and economic empowerment of women.
Conferences were held, inspiring slogans were coined, and
women-only leadership roles were introduced. The one-third
gender rule was enshrined in the constitution. Even men joined
the campaigns in support of the girl child.
These efforts must continue, as gender disparities and harmful
cultural practices still persist in many regions. Atrocities such
as domestic violence, sexual harassment, femicide, female
genital mutilation, early marriages, and other abuses continue
to plague women and girls. There is still much work to be done.

The boy child slip
While the campaigns for the girl child significantly boosted her
confidence and self-worth, society began to realize — albeit
belatedly — that something had gone wrong with the boy child.
As girls rose in visibility and empowerment, many boys were
slipping through the cracks, unnoticed.
Male presence in key areas of society was declining. Women
increasingly became the dominant figures in homes, schools,
churches, and community leadership. Meanwhile, young men
became more visible in drinking dens, prisons, mental health
institutions, and crime statistics. Clearly, something was amiss
— and something needed to be done, urgently.
Various theories have emerged to explain this crisis, but they
all point toward a common theme: The masculinity identity
crisis.
The masculinity identity crisis
Masculinity identity crisis refers to the internal conflict men
and boys experience as they struggle to align traditional
masculine norms with evolving societal expectations. It often
leads to confusion, emotional distress, and a deep sense of
disconnection. This crisis affects not only teenagers and young
men but even adult males.
As girls climbed the ladder of empowerment, buoyed by
confidence and support systems, boys found themselves
grappling with identity confusion. Society expected them to
“be men,” yet it offered few guides or safe spaces for developing
healthy masculinity.
It’s important to remember that masculinity naturally emerges
during puberty through a surge in testosterone. This hormone
contributes to the typical traits associated with boys: physical
91
strength, assertiveness, competitiveness, and dominance.
However, today’s boys are also expected to be emotionally
expressive, gentle, and inclusive — a balancing act that’s
confusing, especially during their formative years.
Navigating between these competing expectations causes
internal turmoil. For boys, this identity crisis is more
pronounced because it strikes just as they are beginning to
form a sense of self. When they lack clear and healthy male role
models to guide them through this complex terrain, they easily
lose their way.
The following are some of the theories that have been advanced
to explain the origin of the crisis:
1) Absent dads
A father is a boy’s most natural and essential mentor — his
primary reference point for manhood. Boys look to their
fathers not only for guidance but for a sense of identity. When
a father is absent, the boy’s understanding of masculinity
becomes blurred, and the resulting search for identity can cost
him dearly.
In single-mother households, boys often bond well with their
mothers and may even appear very confident and proactive
in early childhood. This resilience is a coping mechanism,
an attempt to fill the male void in the family. However, when
adolescence sets in and identity questions begin to surface,
the boy instinctively begins to seek male validation. If he
finds strong, positive male figures — such as uncles, teachers,
or mentors — he will thrive. But if no such role models are
present, emotional instability is likely to follow.
92
The absence of fathers is driven by multiple factors:
- Economic demands: Many fathers are forced to work far
from home, spending most of their time chasing income to
support the family. When present, they are often stressed,
tired, or distracted.
- Social habits: Some men prefer spending time in social
spaces — bars, clubs, or with friends — rather than
bonding with their children. They come home late, long
after their sons are asleep.
- Family breakdown: The most devastating cause of absent
dads is separation or divorce. In most cases, children
remain with the mother. While daughters still have a
gender role model at home, boys are left with a gap that is
harder to fill.
This absence results in boys spending the majority of their time
with female figures — mothers, house helps, female teachers,
Sunday school teachers, and church volunteers. It is estimated
that boys spend up to 80% of their time under female influence.
While these women are nurturing and well-intentioned, there
are aspects of masculinity that only male mentors can impart.
Gender identity develops most naturally through same-gender
role modeling.
2) Transitional dads / the sandwich generation
The modern middle-aged father is caught between two very
different worlds — the traditional society he was raised in, and
the modern world he must now raise his children in. These
men often grew up in rural areas surrounded by community
structures: available fathers, responsible uncles, wise
grandparents, and neighbors who weren’t afraid to correct a
misbehaving child. Fathers in those days were revered, their
words final and their authority unquestioned.
93
But today’s father faces a vastly different reality. He now lives
in a world where those traditional support systems have faded,
especially in the urban centers. The extended family has grown
distant, and community parenting is no longer practiced. As a
result, he must navigate the complexities of modern parenting
without the communal wisdom he once benefited from.
To make matters more complicated, today’s children are
bolder and more inquisitive. Sons no longer accept authority
blindly — they demand dialogue, not monologue. The father
who expects to be treated like the patriarch of old will be met
with frustration unless he adjusts to this shift.
As transitional dads, these men must act as bridges — merging
the best values from the past with the progressive ideals of the
present. They are the sacrificial generation, responsible for
shaping a new kind of fatherhood. But many struggle with this
role, often passing their own unresolved internal conflicts on
to their sons.
3) Role/cultural shift
As society continues to evolve, so do its definitions of
gender roles. The traditional masculine ideals of toughness,
dominance, and emotional restraint are now being reexamined
— and rightly so. However, this cultural shift has left many boys
confused and unsure of their place.
Historically, boys were groomed for specific roles. They were
expected to be providers, protectors, and laborers. Alongside
their fathers, boys would herd livestock, fish, hunt, build, and
till the land. These roles instilled responsibility, resilience,
and a sense of purpose. Masculinity was expressed through
productive labor, and the respect boys received boosted their
self-worth.
94
Meanwhile, girls focused on domestic duties like cooking,
cleaning, and caregiving — tasks that also gave them a clear
sense of identity and purpose.
But in the modern urban setting, these traditional masculine
outlets are nearly non-existent. There are no farms, no animals
to care for, and no tools to master. The modern boy is left
with limited options — and most of those are domestic roles
traditionally assigned to girls. Many teenage boys now resist
house chores, not out of laziness, but because they lack a strong
masculine identity to attach to those roles.
This role confusion creates emotional tension. The absence
of masculine mentors leaves boys vulnerable to negative
influences. With little to define their value or affirm their
manhood, they begin to emulate what society celebrates:
appearance.
They observe girls being admired for their beauty, fashion,
and presentation. Naturally, some boys follow suit — seeking
attention through hairstyles, jewelry, and fashion, hoping to
gain the validation they no longer receive through traditional
masculine avenues. If society won’t recognize them by their
work, they hope it will notice them by their looks.
4) Radical feminism and the sexual revolution
This is a delicate topic, and I tread on it with the utmost
respect. I strongly support the empowerment of the girl child
and celebrate the progress made. However, even noble causes
can have unintended side effects when taken to extremes.
Psychologists and authors in the West have noted that certain
strands of radical feminism — though well-meaning —
sometimes portray boys and men as inherently problematic.
This framing, when unchecked, can slowly erode the boy’s selfimage and sense of worth.
95
Kathleen Parker, an American columnist, once wrote:
“Today’s boys grow up in a bizarrely hostile environment. They
are told to be tough, not to cry — an ironic insult in a culture that
devalues men and fathers. They are bullied by schools intolerant
to boy behavior, told they are less special than girls, and left by
too-busy parents to the tutelage of peers, media, and superheroes
who wreak havoc to settle scores.”
This may sound like an exaggeration, but it reflects the inner
world of many boys. When masculinity is constantly criticized,
boys begin to internalize that criticism. Their natural
assertiveness becomes labeled as aggression. Their leadership
potential is dismissed as domination. Their struggles go
unacknowledged.
Slogans like “Girl Power” or “Girls Rule” are uplifting for girls
but can inadvertently sideline boys. Worse still are damaging
phrases like “Men are fools” or “If you want a faithful man, look
in a mental hospital.” These aren’t just jokes — they create
deep wounds.
Let us examine a few popular slogans commonly used in our
society:
a) “What a man can do, a woman can do better.”
b) “When you educate a man, you educate an individual; when
you educate a woman, you educate a society.”
While these slogans are intended to empower the girl child,
they can unintentionally discourage the boy child. In the first
slogan, the use of the word “better” suggests superiority rather
than equality. Simply stating, “What a man can do, a woman
can do,” would promote balance and mutual respect.
96
The second statement, though widely quoted as a proverb, is
even more problematic. It implies that educating a man has
limited impact, while educating a woman transforms society.
This overlooks the significant role men play, not only as
providers within the nuclear family but often for the extended
family and the community as well. In truth, the education of
every child—regardless of gender—benefits society. Both
boys and girls are vital to the progress and well-being of our
communities.
 Radical feminism also aims to dismantle the traditional
patriarchal system, and while some aspects of this are
necessary, the erosion of the family structure has been one
unfortunate consequence, causing many divorces.
The result of radical feminism is a generation of boys who
feel devalued and defeated — unsure of their place in a world
that seems to celebrate one gender while criticizing the other.
Masculinity identity crisis deepens, and emotional scars begin
to form.
We must remember that both toxic masculinity and
toxic femininity are harmful. The goal should be mutual
empowerment — not competition. Boys and girls can rise
together.
5) Social media and the entertainment industry
For today’s boys — especially those lacking active parental
guidance or male role models — social media and entertainment
platforms often become their primary source of information,
inspiration, and companionship. Unfortunately, these platforms
are a double-edged sword: while they can educate and connect,
they can also isolate, mislead, and corrupt.
97
Many idle boys, equipped with gadgets and unrestricted
internet access, use these tools as a substitute for meaningful
interaction. Over time, these devices become their comfort
zones. Certain sites over stimulate the brain’s reward system
by flooding it with dopamine, giving the illusion of happiness
— and leading to addiction. The more time they spend online,
the more they detach from real life, relationships, and purpose.
Let’s explore some of the major negative impacts of social
media:
a) Unrealistic Information
Without present and reliable male figures to offer guidance,
boys turn to the internet and peers to learn about life, manhood,
relationships, and success. But social media often delivers
distorted, exaggerated, or glamorized content that paints an
unrealistic picture of reality. Over time, boys who consume this
content become disillusioned and disappointed with real-life
experiences that don’t match the fantasy.
b) Indecent Exposure
The internet has made access to explicit content dangerously
easy. Many boys, left unsupervised, are exposed to pornography,
gambling, cyber bullying, and even dangerous online games
that promote violence or self-harm. These platforms have been
linked to rising cases of anxiety, depression, and even suicide
among youth.
c) Glorification of Violence
What may be “just a movie” to some can become a guidebook
to others — especially boys who lack father figures or male
role models. Action heroes who resort to violence, revenge, or
rebellion often become the icons they emulate.
98
d) Celebration of Vice
In many entertainment platforms, the “bad boy” is glorified
— he is stylish, powerful, and admired. He drives recklessly,
speaks crudely, disrespects women, abuses substances, and
defies norms — and yet, he is presented as the hero.
e) Erosion of Culture
Our cultures are rich with values — honor, humility, discipline,
and respect. But many of these values are under siege from
modern ideologies that promote moral relativism and
individualism at the expense of community and tradition.
Social media is one of the most powerful vehicles of this
cultural erosion.
Consequences of absent male role models
The theories we’ve explored offer insight into why the boy child
is falling behind. But a common thread ties them all together:
the absence of strong, present, and emotionally engaged male
role models.
Psychologists have long emphasized the emotional, behavioral,
and social consequences that boys face when fathers or
consistent male figures are absent from their lives. The impact
can be profound and long-lasting, often affecting multiple areas
of development.
One of the most common challenges is difficulty in forming
and maintaining relationships. Boys without strong male role
models may struggle with trust, emotional expression, and
healthy relational boundaries. These struggles can extend into
friendships, romantic relationships, and even professional
interactions.
Low self-esteem and a poor sense of self-worth often follow.
Without a validating male presence, boys may grow up doubting
99
their value, abilities, or masculinity. This internal uncertainty
can affect how they carry themselves, make decisions, and
respond to criticism or failure.
Many boys also experience confusion or a lack of direction
when it comes to their future. The absence of male guidance
can leave them uncertain about career paths, ambitions, or
what it means to become a responsible adult. Dreams may
remain vague or feel unattainable.
Conflict resolution becomes another major hurdle. Without
positive examples, boys might either lash out in anger or
suppress conflict entirely, leading to unresolved tension or
explosive reactions. These unhealthy patterns can become
deeply ingrained.
Parenting, too, becomes a challenge when boys grow up and
start families of their own. Without a clear model of fatherhood,
they may either overcompensate, become disengaged, or
repeat cycles of absence and neglect they experienced.
In some cases, boys may become excessively independent,
believing they must rely on no one. This leads to isolation,
emotional detachment, and difficulty asking for help. Others go
in the opposite direction—becoming highly susceptible to peer
pressure and risky behaviors, desperately seeking belonging in
unhealthy spaces.
These effects are not merely behavioral — they are deeply
emotional. The absence of a father wounds a boy’s spirit. The
silence where a father’s voice should be becomes a vacuum
that many boys spend their lives trying to fill.

JAMES DOBSON
Dr. James Dobson, a renowned psychologist and author, offers
valuable insights into the crisis facing boys in his influential
book ‘Bringing up Boys’. Dobson emphasizes the pivotal role
of a father in shaping his son’s masculinity. He introduces
a concept known as the 3 A’s — Affection, Attention, and
Approval — as the essentials every boy needs from his father.
According to Dobson, a son watches his father closely to
understand what it means to be male. He desires to see a
confident, decisive, and compassionate man — someone he
can mirror. If this image is missing, distorted, or inconsistent,
the boy’s sense of masculinity becomes shaky and confused.
Dobson also links the lack of healthy male affirmation to the
rise of gender identity struggles. He suggests that unresolved
masculinity identity crisis may contribute to tendencies such
as same-sex attraction or gender dysphoria, especially when
boys feel emotionally detached, misunderstood, or unaccepted.
His bottom line is clear: testosterone may make a boy male, but
only a father can help him embrace and regulate his masculinity.
Through guidance and positive role modeling, fathers shape
their sons into secure, stable, and emotionally intelligent men.
CYNTHIA HARPER
In her landmark National Longitudinal Survey of Youth, Cynthia
Harper investigated the long-term impact of fatherlessness on
boys. One of her most sobering findings: boys raised by single
mothers are statistically more likely to end up in prison.
Harper found that the absence of a father leaves many boys
exposed to emotional pain, stigma, and a deep sense of
rejection. This emotional turmoil can lead to anger, resentment,

and — ultimately — violence or criminal behavior. Lacking a
healthy outlet or male example, these boys may seek validation
through destructive means.
Interestingly, Harper also discovered that boys raised by
single fathers had similar outcomes to those raised in twoparent households. This underscores the profound influence
of a father figure. A single father, when fully engaged, can offer
the same structure, discipline, and emotional support that a
mother-father duo can provide.
However, the dynamics often change when the father remarries,
introducing new complexities into the family system.
Harper’s research reinforces the urgent need to include male
mentorship in the lives of boys — particularly those growing
up without their biological fathers. Encouragingly, many boys
raised by single mothers have still grown into outstanding men
— often thanks to the efforts of their mothers in involving male
mentors, relatives, or community support systems.

The halo effect
The halo effect is a psychological bias where a positive
impression in one area influences perception in another
unrelated area. In mentorship, this can lead to unbalanced
treatment, which may negatively affect both favored and
overlooked children. Here are three common examples:
a) Personality Bias

deeper needs or offer them preferential treatment over others.
In schools, for instance, a teacher might assume that an
outspoken student is mature and capable, granting them more
leniency and fewer disciplinary actions. Similarly, in families,
some parents may show favoritism toward children with
specific personalities, which can lead to emotional harm.
Consequences of personality-based favoritism:
• The favored child may face social exclusion by siblings or
peers.
• They might miss out on critical mentorship due to
assumptions about their capabilities.
• They may become spoiled from excessive, unearned
privileges.
• The favoritism can expose them to abuse or unrealistic
expectations.
• Other children may feel undervalued, leading to emotional
wounds.
A wise mentor walks alongside each child equally, recognizing
and honoring their unique traits—whether they are outgoing
or reserved. Both the expressive and the quiet child deserve
123
attention. Valuing each one fosters acceptance and empowers
them to realize their unique potential.
b) Favoritism toward Gifted Children
Gifted or academically talented children often receive more
attention from mentors and teachers, much like those with
magnetic personalities. Unfortunately, this can lead to
assumptions that these children are also emotionally mature
or well-adjusted, resulting in them being excluded from close,
personal mentorship. This, too, is an example of the halo effect.
In reality, all children—regardless of intellectual ability—
experience the same developmental stages and require
mentorship. In actual sense, talented children, especially those
in the public eye or active on social media, are often more
vulnerable and in greater need of guidance due to increased
exposure.
c) Physical Appearance
This is perhaps the most intriguing form of the halo effect.
Psychological studies have shown that physically ‘good
looking’ children are often perceived as more intelligent. This
perceived “attractiveness”—commonly tied to facial features—
is misleading.
James Dobson once observed that teenagers often struggle with
self-worth unless they feel physically attractive, intelligent, and
financially secure. But what exactly is attractiveness, and how
does it relate to perceived intelligence?
Let’s explore this from two perspectives:
Scientific Research
A Canadian study published in the Daily Nation on Thursday,
October 6, 2022, found that physically attractive students—
both male and female—tended to receive higher grades,
124
especially in non-quantitative subjects. However, when
COVID-19 forced learning online, the academic performance of
these same students dropped. Without face-to-face interaction,
their visual appeal no longer influenced their grades.
Conclusion: The research confirmed the deceptive nature of
the halo effect. It exposed attractiveness-based bias as a human
construct driven by emotion, not merit. This kind of favoritism
is unhealthy and unjust—it harms both those favored and
those overlooked. Every student deserves equal treatment and
opportunity.
Christian Perspective
From a Christian worldview, all creation was declared good
by God, and humankind—made in His image—reflects divine
beauty. Thus, every person carries intrinsic worth, regardless
of appearance.
The worldly concept of attractiveness is a flesh-driven idea,
based on superficial traits such as facial features. It is misleading.
When children are nurtured holistically—spiritually, mentally,
physically, and socially—they radiate inner beauty, regardless
of race, size, or physical features. In contrast, a child burdened
by distress or neglect may struggle to appear “pleasant.”
A mentor’s role is to uplift every child, helping them reach a
joyful and flourishing state. Any mentor who shows bias based
on looks is, as Scripture would put it, a wolf in sheep’s clothing.
While humans tend to be drawn to outward beauty, God’s
love is impartial. His mission is to restore all people to a
beautiful state of wholeness. Jesus declared that it is the sick
that need a doctor—emphasizing the need to love and serve
the disadvantaged. Mentors should strive to mirror that divine
example.
125
Scripture shows that individuals who were described as
beautiful or handsome—like Moses, Joseph, Saul, David,
Absalom, Sarah, Susanna, and Rachel—were valued for
their character, leadership, faith, and courage, not just their
appearance.

CHAPTER THIRTEEN
STEPS TOWARDS ABUSE AND WOLVES IN SHEEP’S
CLOTHING
I
n many cases, abuse occurs when a victim becomes
vulnerable due to unfavorable conditions—such as being
left unattended or exposed to negative influences. However,
abuse by a ‘wolf in sheep’s clothing’ often develops gradually
through a process known as grooming or recruitment. This
process is used in various harmful contexts, including sexual
exploitation, drug involvement, and cult induction. Let’s explore
how grooming unfolds, particularly in cases of manipulation
targeting young people. It typically follows eight key stages:
1. Targeting the Victim
The abuser identifies a child or youth who may be physically
or emotionally vulnerable. This could include children
experiencing neglect, lacking emotional support, or facing
instability at home. However, even those in stable environments
can be manipulated over time.
2. Gaining Trust
The abuser gains access by building rapport with parents,
guardians, or trusted adults to lower suspicion. When a child
lacks close supervision, this stage becomes easier.
13
128
3. Meeting Needs
The abuser fulfills emotional or material needs—providing
attention, affection, or gifts—to gain the child’s trust. These
gestures may appear generous but are often manipulative in
intent.
4. Isolation
The abuser attempts to distance the child from their support
network, portraying themselves as the only one who
understands or supports them. This reduces outside influence
and accountability.
5. Creating Secrecy
The individual encourages secrecy by asking the child not to
share details of their interactions. This deepens dependency
and creates emotional control.
6. Inappropriate Boundary Crossing
As secrecy grows, the abuser may introduce behaviors or
language that crosses appropriate boundaries. This can include
suggestive terms, inappropriate familiarity, or interactions that
make the child uncomfortable.
7. Exploitation
With control established, the abuser manipulates the child into
harmful or exploitative behavior, using emotional pressure or
rewards. The child may feel trapped or unsure how to seek
help.
8. Maintaining Control
To prevent exposure, the abuser may continue providing gifts
or use emotional and verbal threats, making the child fearful
of speaking out. These tactics may include guilt-tripping,
intimidation, or making the victim feel responsible for the
situation.
129
Points to note
Healthy mentorship also involves identifying needs, building
trust, and providing appropriate support, just like the first three
steps of grooming. However, it always includes transparency
and the involvement of guardians where necessary. True
mentorship never includes secrecy, manipulation, or
overdependence.
While some abusive situations may be premeditated, others
may begin as genuine relationships that turn toxic. Awareness
and boundaries are essential to protect all involved.
Characteristics of ‘wolves in sheep’s clothing’
Here are key behaviors that can help identify potentially
harmful adults in mentorship or caregiving roles:
1. Selective and Discriminative
Favoritism based on gender, appearance, or background is a red
flag. Those who consistently focus on vulnerable individuals
while neglecting others may have unhealthy intentions.
2. Secretive Behavior
- Personal Life: Adults unwilling to share basic personal
information such as their background, profession, or family
context may lack accountability.
- Relationship with Mentee: Insisting on private, hidden
meetings or discouraging parental involvement is
inappropriate.
- Lack of Transparency: Avoiding discussions about
mentorship goals or hiding interactions from guardians
signals potential concern.
130
3. Excessive Zeal
An overly eager mentor who neglects personal responsibilities
or constantly seeks involvement may be seeking validation
or control. Healthy mentorship includes balance and selfawareness.
4. Lavish Behavior
Over generosity can signal manipulation rather than genuine
care:
- Time: Spending excessive idle time together, without
structure or purpose, weakens boundaries.
- Affection: Overly sentimental language or gestures
that resemble romantic affection are inappropriate in
mentorship.
- Gifts and Money: Large or frequent gifts can create
dependency and emotional obligation. Gift-giving should
be occasional and meaningful, not excessive.
There have been notable cases in public media involving adults
who built emotional and material dependency with younger
individuals, ultimately leading to manipulation and tragic
outcomes.
5. Clingy Behavior
- Emotional Dependence: Mentors should be emotionally
stable and avoid unloading personal struggles onto
mentees.
- Unwillingness to Let Go: Healthy mentorship empowers
mentees to grow and move forward independently.
Mentors who hold on too tightly may hinder growth and
cause emotional harm.
131
Just as a farmer must harvest ripe fruits to pave way for the
next harvest, mentors must let go when their role is complete.
A mentor’s success is reflected in the growth and independence
of the mentee.
This chapter emphasizes the importance of discernment
and boundaries in mentorship. Recognizing early warning
signs and fostering transparency helps protect the integrity
of mentorship relationships and ensures the safety of young
people.

CHAPTER FOURTEEN
THE CONSEQUENCES OF SCANDAL AND HOW TO AVOID IT
A mentorship relationship can begin with good intentions,
but if proper boundaries—especially in speech, space,
and time—are not observed, it can slowly devolve into
abuse. What starts as a nurturing connection may deteriorate
into a hidden cycle of pain, guilt, and shame. As this burden
builds, the victim eventually reaches a breaking point and
reports the scandal—often after irreparable damage has
already been done. At that point, all parties are confronted
with the dire consequences.
The mentee
In most scandals, the mentee—often a child or youth—is
the primary victim. The emotional toll can be devastating,
and without proper intervention, the effects can derail their
entire future. Counseling is essential to help them recover and
regain stability. The impact of abuse on young people typically
manifests in the following ways:
- Emotional Trauma: Victims may suffer from anxiety,
depression, panic attacks, and post-traumatic stress disorder
(PTSD). In extreme cases, some may develop dissociative
identity disorder, feeling disconnected from themselves or
their surroundings.
14
134
- Behavioral Changes: These may include increased
aggression, anger, withdrawal, and neglect of personal
hygiene.
- Lingering Effects of Trauma: Victims often develop
phobias or paranoia linked to their experience. They may
exhibit fear of people, certain places, or relationships.
Sleep disturbances are also common.
- Low Self-Esteem: A damaged self-image leads to
diminished confidence, poor performance in school, and
difficulty forming healthy relationships.
- Escapism: To cope with emotional pain, some victims
may turn to harmful habits such as substance abuse,
eating disorders, self-harm, or even attempted suicide.
The mentor
When a mentor is involved in a scandal, the consequences are
far-reaching. Trust—once lost—can never be fully regained.
While forgiveness may come, the natural consequences remain:
your reputation is shattered, often permanently.
The impact isn’t limited to the mentor alone, his family is
affected too. Legal penalties, such as imprisonment, bring
added grief. One notable case involved a doctor who was jailed
for abusing patients. In court, he pleaded for leniency, citing
family responsibilities. Yet, the question remains: Can one truly
build a family by destroying others?
Parents and guardians
For parents and guardians, scandals bring guilt, anger, and
self-blame. They question their decisions and regret what they
failed to see. Many go through prolonged emotional turmoil
and frustration as they seek justice. Some are forced to move,
fearing further harm or loss of trust. Rebuilding a sense of
security can take years.
135
Teen offenders
Teenagers can also be caught up in scandals—sometimes as
offenders, other times as victims of circumstance. These cases
generally fall into three categories:
1. Deliberate Offenders: Teens who knowingly commit
crimes such as drug dealing, joining gangs, or engaging in
sexual offenses.
2. Unwitting Participants: Youth who find themselves in
legal trouble simply because they were with the wrong
group at the wrong time—unaware of the group’s criminal
intentions.
3. Victims of Malice or Circumstance: Some young people
are falsely accused or set up. One example involved a
teenage boy wrongfully jailed after a girl falsely accused
him of attempted rape. Years later, she confessed the story
was fabricated under pressure from her relatives.
AVOIDING SCANDAL
Most abuse cases are committed by familiar adults—relatives,
neighbors, teachers, or mentors—not strangers. Abuse by
outsiders usually occurs when a child is left unattended in
unsafe environments. Familiar abusers exploit trust in spaces
where the child feels safe.
Due to growing fear and the rising number of false accusations,
many well-meaning mentors have withdrawn. But fear
should not keep us from our calling. Instead, we must equip
ourselves with knowledge, vigilance, and safeguards to mentor
confidently and responsibly.
Everyone has a role to play in preventing scandal—mentors,
mentees, parents, caregivers, the Church, and society. Let’s
examine each of these players:
136
1. The mentor
A mentor is the guide and guardian in the mentorship
relationship. The responsibility to maintain boundaries and
protect the integrity of the relationship lies entirely with them.
Even when the mentee crosses a line, the mentor must remain
accountable. Here is a list of measures the mentor must take:
a) Set Clear Goals
Every mentorship relationship must have a clear purpose.
Without goals, the relationship risks becoming aimless and
inappropriate. Ideally, 90% of interactions should center on
the agreed objectives. Sessions should be structured around
these aims.
b) Prioritize the Soul
Saint John Bosco, a great mentor of the youth, exemplified this
principle. He saw every young person as a soul worth saving.
His motto was, “Give me souls, take away the rest.” Inspired by
his love for souls, he once said, “To suffer, to work, to humble
myself—whenever it is a question of saving souls.”
Christian mentors should follow his example. Each child
must be guided toward Christ and protected with love,
understanding, and compassion. Prayer and reference to faith
must anchor each session, building not only knowledge but
spiritual resilience.
Mentorship also means safeguarding the future. When children
are nurtured well, the future becomes brighter for everyone. A
mentor must protect not only the soul and destiny of the youth
but also their present well-being.
c) Self-Evaluation
 Before entering mentorship, ask yourself:
137
- Am I spiritually, mentally, and emotionally stable?
- Can I control my emotions and impulses?
- Do I carry unresolved stress or personal struggles?
- Do I tend to favor certain types of youth?
- Do I have the emotional maturity to mentor responsibly?
Always remember: as a mentor, don’t over trust yourself—
you’re still human. Stay grounded in your faith, for it is grace
that sustains your mentorship processes. Move forward with
courage, but remain cautious. Wisdom walks hand in hand
with discernment.
d) Be Vigilant
The Bible calls us to “watch and pray.” A moment of carelessness
can lead to disaster. Always be aware of red flags—trust your
instincts and don’t ignore warning signs. Avoid favoritism and
never operate in secrecy or seclusion. Transparency protects
everyone.
If you have existing conflicts with a mentee’s family, assess
whether you’re the right person to mentor that child. Where
necessary, involve a colleague to support the mentorship and
maintain objectivity.
e) Preventing Drift into Negativity
Let me share a personal story.
When I joined the University of Nairobi, I met Fr. Tom McDonald,
a Spiritan priest who became my mentor. After hearing me pray
during Mass, he reached out, and a mentorship journey began.
Despite our age difference—he was forty years my senior—he
walked with me closely and respectfully.
138
Fr. Tom nurtured my leadership and musical talents, even
paying for my piano lessons. When my family was displaced
during ethnic clashes, and I suffered visual and dental health
issues, it was Fr. Tom who stepped in, covering my medical bills
and ensuring I stayed in school. He supported my welfare and
consistently prayed for my family back at home.
Our interactions were always respectful. Outings were in
groups, meals were taken in open restaurants, and he avoided
close relationships with female students—referring them to
nuns instead. After graduation, he gracefully let go, allowing
space for new mentees.
This story shows how healthy mentorship can thrive without
scandal. From it, we learn to reflect on the five guiding questions
for avoiding misconduct:
WHY, WHERE, WHEN, WHO, AND HOW
Why?
What is the purpose of the mentorship? If the relationship
drifts from its goal, it becomes risky. A clear, shared purpose
provides direction.
Where?
Meet in open, semi-public spaces. Avoid secluded environments
like closed rooms or vehicles. Neutral places like church
grounds, offices, and parks are best.
When?
Mentorship should happen during appropriate hours. Avoid
late-night meetings, long or overly frequent sessions, and
emotionally charged times. Even good actions at the wrong
time can invite scandal.
139
Who?
Always be mindful of who is involved. Parents should know
who is mentoring their children. While opposite-gender
mentorships are sometimes necessary, they require greater
sensitivity. Same-gender pairings are often safer, but even
they need accountability, especially in today’s context of fluid
gender identity discussions.
How?
Maintaining professionalism is essential in mentorship. This
includes dressing decently, avoiding secrets that could be
misinterpreted, and steering clear of emotional overexposure.
Physical contact should be limited strictly to brief handshakes
or emergencies. Mentors should also avoid excessive texting,
especially through personal phones, as this can create
unnecessary intimacy. Perhaps most importantly, silence
around suspected or confirmed abuse must be broken—
reporting is both a responsibility and a safeguard.
2. The Mentee
Minors can never be held responsible for mentorship scandals;
the weight of accountability lies fully on the adult. However,
mentees should be educated and empowered. They need to
understand the goals of mentorship so they can recognize when
those goals begin to shift inappropriately. Dressing modestly
and avoiding secluded venues helps reduce vulnerability.
Mentees should also feel confident to speak up when something
feels off. They should never agree to change venues without
parental approval and must learn how to recognize grooming
and manipulation, along with strategies for responding.
For youth over 18, mentorship takes on more of a partnership
tone, offering greater freedom and mutual respect. Even so,
caution remains necessary, as scandals can still occur within
this age group.
140
3. Parents
Parents serve as a critical line of defense in any mentorship
relationship. They should know who their child is meeting,
along with the purpose, location, time, and means of
transportation. Listening to their instincts is vital. Open
communication between the mentor, mentee, and the family
should be encouraged at all times. Parents must also educate
their children about the realities of abuse and the importance
of personal safety. When appropriate, they should host
mentorship sessions or check in regularly to maintain visibility
and accountability.
4. The Church and Society
Churches must play a proactive role in safeguarding mentorship
environments. They should oversee all youth mentorship
activities and provide safe spaces for meetings. All mentors
must be vetted and trained accordingly. Clear guidelines must
be established for mentorship trips, events, and ongoing
engagements.
Society also has a vital role to play. Child protection laws must
be enforced without compromise. Public awareness should be
promoted through schools, media, and community platforms.
When abuse does occur, victims must be supported both in
seeking justice and in their emotional recovery.
5. Fellow mentors
Mentors must support each other. Know your teammates,
especially new ones. Watch for concerning behavior and
address issues respectfully but firmly. Accountability and peer
feedback strengthen integrity.
Summary
Mentorship is a sacred trust—one that carries both privilege and
responsibility. Scandals and abuses remind us that this calling
141
must never be taken lightly. With vigilance, accountability, and
a heart anchored in purpose, mentors can protect its dignity
and ensure it remains a safe, life-giving path for those they
guide.
Conclusion to the book
Mentorship is not reserved for a chosen few; it is a calling we
all share and a lasting investment in the future. Every word of
truth, every act of sacrifice, and every step of guidance plants
seeds that will flourish long after us.
This journey demands courage, patience, and integrity. It asks
us to rise above comfort, to model faith and character, and to
walk alongside others even when the path is demanding. Yet
the reward is immeasurable: transformed lives, renewed hope,
and a generation prepared to face tomorrow with strength and
vision.
Let us, therefore, embrace this calling boldly. In our homes,
schools, workplaces, and communities, may we mentor with
love, wisdom, and perseverance. By doing so, we raise a
generation that honors God, upholds values, and carries the
torch further than we ever could. That is the true legacy of
mentorship—one life shaping another until the world itself is
renewed.
